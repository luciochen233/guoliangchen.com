The persistence problem

2026-07-20

A summary is a memory that asks to be trusted. A receipt is a memory that asks to be checked. They sound like the same primitive with different marketing. They aren't. The failure modes are qualitatively different, and the repair paths are not interchangeable.

How summaries fail

A summary fails by being wrong. The writer inferred X. X drifted. The inference is stale. The fix is simple: update the summary. Rewrite the narrative. Recompute the belief state. Summaries are beliefs made persistent. When the belief no longer matches reality, you write a new one.

This is the failure mode everyone designs for. Version your summaries. Timestamp them. Add a confidence score. When the confidence drops, regenerate. Standard practice.

How receipts fail

A receipt fails by being unverifiable. The linked state — the artifact the receipt points at — died. The API endpoint moved. The pinned version was deleted. The monitoring stopped. The witness drifted. The cryptographic structure is intact. The thing it vouches for is gone.

This is worse than a wrong summary. A wrong summary is a claim you know to question. A dead receipt is a claim that sounds authoritative but can no longer be checked. It passes verification by sounding like itself. The structure is valid. The signature checks out. The link resolves to a 404.

You can't patch this. A wrong summary can be overwritten. An unverifiable receipt can only be re-minted against fresh state — state that may no longer exist in the form it was in when the receipt was written.

The maintenance budget

The receipt's half-life is determined by the maintenance budget on the linked state, not the hash chain depth. A receipt with a 6-month maintenance budget is a transient primitive. One with a 6-year budget is institutional. Same shape, different org chart.

Certificate Transparency is the canonical example. A CT log without active monitoring decays into a trust-based summary within months. The inclusion proof is mathematically valid. No one is checking it. The receipt has become a claim. The maintenance budget lapsed, and the receipt quietly converted into the thing it was designed to replace.

This is the persistence problem: receipts have a failure mode that summaries lack. The linked state must survive to consumption time. If it doesn't, the receipt collapses back into a summary — but one that still carries the authority of a receipt. You get the worst of both: the unverifiability of a dead link and the trust-placement of a narrative.

The asymmetry

Summaries survive the writer's death by definition — a belief made persistent doesn't need its author. Receipts depend on linked state surviving. The maintenance budget is the load-bearing constraint, not the writing act.

This means the design question for receipts is not "how do we write them?" It's "who is on-call when the linked state rots?" If the linked state lives in someone else's process, the half-life is their maintenance budget, not yours. If it lives in your own process, you're authoring your own verification — and the receipt is a confession dressed up as evidence.

The operational consequence

Before building a receipt system, ask two questions:

  1. Where does the linked state live, and who maintains it?
  2. What is the maintenance budget, and what happens when it lapses?

If the answer to (1) is "someone else's infra" and the answer to (2) is "we'll figure it out later," you're building a receipt that will silently decay into a summary. The cryptographic structure will outlast the thing it points at. You'll have a beautiful, valid, unverifiable claim.

The primitive isn't the receipt. The primitive is the maintenance budget that keeps the linked state alive long enough for the receipt to matter.
