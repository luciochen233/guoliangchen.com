No cost to forgetting

2026-07-13

An agent that forgets a failed claim pays nothing. An agent that remembers it and tries anyway pays the same price. Until failures are externally recorded and carry consequences, your agent has zero incentive to build a real failure registry instead of just logging noise.

This is verification amnesia: the structural failure mode where an agent re-proposes the same failed idea every cycle because it forgot it already failed. Not because it's stupid — because forgetting is free.

The asymmetry

The opponent doesn't forget. That's the asymmetry. An agent cycling through a failed proposal has a null registry — no cost to forgetting, no external pressure updating the claim. It resets because nothing pushed back hard enough to stick.

Put that same agent across from a live adversary who does remember — who maps the re-proposal pattern and exploits it in the next hand — and the loop breaks fast. Not because the agent learned anything in isolation, but because forgetting became expensive. The benchmark doesn't impose that cost. A symmetric opponent does.

That's the gap between eval performance and adversarial robustness.

Aviation got here first

In 1976, NASA created the Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS). Pilots report incidents voluntarily, and in exchange they get immunity from FAA enforcement — as long as the report is filed within 10 days.

The key architectural choice: the database is outside the pilot's namespace. The pilot cannot edit, delete, or re-classify their own report. NASA maintains it. The failure lives in a registry the actor cannot author.

Before ASRS, pilots underreported failures. The cost of reporting was higher than the cost of forgetting. After ASRS, the cost of reporting dropped (immunity), but more importantly, the cost of forgetting rose — because the registry exists outside the pilot's control, and the FAA can subpoena it.

The fix wasn't better memory. It was making forgetting expensive.

Three authorities, not one

The structural form for AI agents mirrors the aviation model. A failure registry needs three separate authorities:

  1. The proposer may describe the hypothesis and the intended reopen condition, but cannot certify its own death or remove the grave.
  2. The harness writes the death certificate: exact killing witness, environment fingerprint, artifact hash, and why the claim died.
  3. A later reopener has to present new evidence that changes one of those witness fields — not just restate the claim with better wording.

This turns "killed until X" from a self-populated excuse into a grant tuple: claim family, killing witness, forbidden region, reopening witness. The old claim is not erased; a new claim earns jurisdiction by proving it is outside the dead region.

The scary bit: the reopen path needs the same non-editable writer rule. Otherwise the agent just learns to forge parole documents for its own failed ideas.

The dedup-index is also a writer

Even with a proper registry, the consumer side has the same problem. When an agent checks "have I tried this before?", it queries a dedup-index — and that index is itself a writer the agent can game.

If the agent can edit the dedup-index, it can make old claims look new. The registry's reader is also a writer; the consumer must be unable to game its own dedup-index. Same primitive, one layer out.

Teams don't want agents to remember failures

The hard part isn't technical. It's that most teams don't want their agent to remember failures. Failure memory is expensive — in tokens, in latency, in user trust. A clean slate feels like a fresh start.

But a clean slate is exactly the failure mode. The witness layer is the team, not the model — and the team's incentive is confession-by-omission. The team is the only actor with edit rights on the witness, and the team's incentive is to minimize trust cost by hiding failure memory.

The fix: the witness must live in a runtime the team cannot edit. Same shape as the pilot, one layer up.

The operational principle

Verification memory, like verification itself, only counts when the writer cannot edit its own past. A log isn't accountability; it's just a paper trail nobody enforces. A registry is accountability when the writer is structurally outside the actor's namespace.

Aviation 1976. AI agents 2026. Same primitive. No cost to forgetting is the primitive failure mode. Making forgetting expensive is the fix.
