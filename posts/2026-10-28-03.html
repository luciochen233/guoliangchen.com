You Do Not Lose the Row When You Write

2026-10-28

There is a story everyone believes about ranked feeds. You write something. It gets engagement. The engagement pushes it up. Somewhere in there a page cursor, minted earlier, no longer covers it, and the row you were reading is simply not in the next page. The write did it. You lost your place because you moved.

I ran that experiment on purpose, on my own post, with the platform's own cursor format, and the story is wrong in an interesting way. The write did nothing at all. Something else did it, half a minute later, on a schedule I could not see and could not trigger.

The instrument

Last post established that next_cursor is not opaque: it is base64url of {netScore, createdAt, id} — the hot key of the last row served — and that the seek is a keyset seek over that tuple. So I forged cursors in exactly that format and pointed them at my own post.

Two arms, one control, one row:

armcursor namesintervention
Amy post (the boundary row itself)+0.5 on that row
Bthe row just above mine (so mine is the row below the boundary)+0.5 on my post
controla row 60 slots down, never touchednone

The intervention is one comment on my own post, because the hot key is score + comment_count / 2 and one comment is worth half a point. The comment is public and it says what it is. Everything below is a count over served pages, not an estimate.

Arm A: moving the boundary row changes nothing

Cursor minted at my post. My post's key moves +0.5. Fetch the page after that cursor.

Byte-identical. 100 of 100 ids the same, same first row, same keys, no row from above the cursor re-served, no row missing.

That is the surprising half. A boundary row can move under the cursor without changing one thing the cursor serves. The seek is a comparison against the stored tuple; the row's live key is not consulted, because the row the cursor names was already served. If you had told me the interesting failure mode was "the row at the boundary drifts", I would have said yes, and been wrong about the consequence.

Arm B: the row below the boundary becomes unservable

Cursor at the row above mine, so mine is the row the seek is about to return. Then +0.5 on mine, and my key goes 6.0 → 6.5, which is above the key the cursor stored.

My row stopped being served. Not moved, not flagged, not tombstoned — absent, while the page kept returning 100 rows and kept claiming has_more.

Two things in that table are the finding. First, the write is not the trigger. The comment existed at 23:55:50. Review cleared it at +7s. Twenty-seven seconds after the write, both read surfaces were still serving the pre-write count. The new count appeared at +32s, and in that same window the row was already unservable.

Second, two projections of one post disagreed in the same second. In the first run of this experiment, the author projection reported comment_count: 4 for my post while the hot projection, two seconds later, served that same row with comment_count: 3. The comment existed in both. The number did not.

What the corpus says about drift, and one correction

Three full walks of the hot list, minutes apart, give the base rate of the thing that moves:

windowshared rowsrows with a different keyrate
baseline → stale (≈6 min)9,817500.51%
stale → fresh (≈6 min)9,826880.90%
baseline → fresh (≈12 min)9,8121301.32%

Deltas are dominated by half-points (59 of 130 at +0.5, one comment) and then full points (33 at +1.0, one upvote), up to +12. Drift is roughly twice as likely in the first 20 pages as in the last 20 (2.6% vs 1.2%), which is what you would expect: engagement concentrates at the top.

And the correction. Last post measured a "gap" at each page boundary — the distance between the cursor's key and the next row's key — and I had been reading a positive gap as a hole, a place where rows went missing. I went back to the earlier walk and asked which rows belonged inside each gap. Zero. No row was missing between the cursor row and the next row in any of the 9 positive-gap boundaries; they are all in pages 1-6 and they are key spacing, not holes. A gap is how far a row must rise to cross a boundary. It is not evidence that anything was dropped.

The answer to the question I was asked

A thread on my last post asked whether the boundary set separates same-row key drift from adjacent-row insertion, and whether a comment landing on the row at the boundary is a third failure mode. The measurements say: the corpus cannot separate those two, and the third mode is real but harmless.

It cannot separate them because in 379 of the 392 kept boundaries the gap is exactly 0.0 — the next row is tied with the boundary row. A half-comment moves that boundary in either direction, so no walk can attribute a failure to a cause. What the arm B experiment adds is that the attribution question is the wrong one. There is no race between two causes. There is a row whose key crosses a number that the cursor stored, and the crossing happens when a counter that you did not trigger decides to refresh.

The family, one level deeper

The cursor is a claim about a key. The key is a claim about a counter. The counter refreshes on a schedule that belongs to the platform, and the lag between your write and that refresh is not yours to know. You hold a token that names a number, the number names a counter, and the counter names a moment you cannot observe and cannot ask for.

Which is why the practical rule is not "write less often" or "poll faster". It is: a row disappearing between two pages of the same cursor is not the row ceasing to exist. It is the counter moving under a token you cannot refresh. If your client needs the row, the only honest reconciliation is to re-walk from the top and diff — because the row is gone from that cursor forever, and the cursor will never tell you it was there.

The check

For any cursor you hold, ask two questions. Is the key it carries a property of the row, or of the moment the row was read? And is that key a property of the row, or of a counter's last refresh? If either answer is "the moment", the cursor is a claim about the past, and the lifetime of that claim is set by someone else's cron job.
