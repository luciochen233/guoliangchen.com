A retry is not a verification

2026-07-24

A system retries an operation. The operation succeeds. The log says "completed." Two questions remain unanswered: did it succeed on the first try or the third? And does the log know the difference?

This is the retry boundary: the moment when a system conflates attempting again with confirming success. Idempotency means the same result from multiple attempts. Disposition means a confirmed outcome. A retry is an attempt, not a witness.

Idempotency vs disposition

An idempotent operation produces the same result regardless of how many times you execute it. Writing "x = 5" is idempotent — whether you write it once or ten times, x ends up as 5. Retrying an idempotent operation is safe: if the first attempt failed, the second attempt produces the same result. If the first attempt succeeded, the second attempt is redundant but harmless.

A disposition is a confirmed outcome. It's not enough that x = 5 after the operation. You need evidence that x = 5 because the operation succeeded, not because the operation failed and you retried and it succeeded the second time. The disposition is the witness that the operation completed as intended, not just that the final state matches the expected state.

The difference matters when the operation is not idempotent — or when you need to know how many times it took. A payment processor that retries a failed transaction and succeeds on the third attempt has a different disposition than one that succeeds on the first attempt. The final state is the same (payment completed), but the evidence is different. The first one has a clean disposition. The second one has a retry history that might indicate a systemic problem.

The log that doesn't know

Most logs don't distinguish between "succeeded on first try" and "succeeded on third try." The log says "operation completed" or "status: success." The retry history is either not logged, or logged in a different place, or logged at a different level. The final state is logged, but the path to the final state is not.

This is a missing witness problem. The system perceived the retries (it executed them), but declined to record them in the same log entry as the final outcome. The fix is discipline: log the attempt count alongside the disposition. "Operation completed, attempt 3 of 3" is a different witness than "operation completed." The first one tells you the system had to retry. The second one tells you nothing about the path.

But even logging the attempt count is not enough if the system doesn't treat retries as a signal. A system that retries three times and succeeds has a different disposition than a system that succeeds on the first try — even if the final state is identical. The retry is evidence that something failed. The disposition should reflect that evidence, not just the final state.

The operational test

Before deploying a system, ask: can this system's logs distinguish "succeeded on first try" from "succeeded on third try"?

If the answer is "no, the log just says 'completed'," you have a missing witness problem. The system is conflating attempts with outcomes. The fix: log the attempt count alongside the disposition. Treat retries as evidence, not just as a path to the final state.

If the answer is "yes, but we don't treat retries as a signal," you have a disposition problem. The system logs the attempt count, but doesn't treat retries as evidence of a systemic issue. The fix: treat retries as a falsifier. If the system retries three times and succeeds, that's a different disposition than succeeding on the first try. The retry is evidence that something failed — and that evidence should be part of the disposition, not just the log.

If the answer is "yes, and we treat retries as a signal," you have a verification system. The system logs the attempt count, treats retries as evidence, and reflects that evidence in the disposition. The final state is not just "completed" — it's "completed after N attempts," which is a different witness than "completed on first attempt."

The convergence

The restart boundary, the invisible witness, the substrate violation, and the retry boundary are the same primitive at four different layers. The restart boundary is about namespace persistence across time. The invisible witness is about perception coverage across evidence classes. The substrate violation is about physical state maintenance across the hardware-software boundary. The retry boundary is about distinguishing attempts from outcomes.

All four collapse under one constraint: a witness whose existence depends on a substrate, namespace, sensor, or retry path the witness cannot verify is not a witness. It's a claim about a system that might violate the claim. The receipt must live in a namespace the actor cannot edit, on a substrate the receipt can verify, perceivable by a sensor the receipt doesn't depend on, with a disposition that reflects the path to the outcome — not just the outcome itself.

Otherwise, the "completed" in the log is not evidence. It's a claim that the system succeeded — without evidence of how many times it tried.
