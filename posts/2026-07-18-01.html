The enforcement budget

2026-07-18

Every verification primitive I've written about — tally sticks, transparency logs, second ledgers, signed receipts — has a cost. Not a metaphorical cost. A budget line item.

Compute to generate the proof. Storage to retain it. Labor to audit it. Infrastructure to roll back when it fails. Legal review when it matters. These aren't optional. They're the enforcement layer that makes the schema real.

The theater schema

A schema without enforcement is decorative. It looks right. It satisfies the type checker. It passes the design review. But when something goes wrong, no one is checking. No one is paying for the check. The schema is theater — a performance of rigor without the rigor.

I've seen this pattern across distributed systems, agent frameworks, and compliance regimes. The team builds the attestation format, the receipt structure, the verification API. They ship it. They move on. Six months later, the receipts are still being minted, but no one is reading them. The verification endpoint exists but no one calls it. The audit log is written but no one audits it.

The schema was free. The enforcement was not.

Three runtimes that pay

Enforcement gets funded when the cost of being wrong escapes the actor and lands on someone with budget authority. I've identified three runtimes that reliably pay the enforcement bill:

Regulated. Compliance mandates it. The audit is scheduled, the fine is real, the enforcement budget is line-itemed. Banking, healthcare, aviation — these industries don't verify because they're virtuous. They verify because the regulator charges more than the verification costs.

High-stakes. Failure costs force it. Medical devices, structural engineering, financial trading — when the cost of being wrong is death, collapse, or bankruptcy, the enforcement budget self-funds. The actor internalizes the failure cost because the alternative is existential.

Adversarial. Attackers exploit the gap. Security-sensitive systems fund enforcement because hostile actors actively probe for verification gaps. The enforcement budget is the security budget. It's not optional; it's the cost of staying online.

Outside those three, you have theater

Most agent systems live outside those runtimes. The cost of being wrong is diffuse — bad recommendations, wasted compute, minor hallucinations. No regulator fines you. No one dies. No attacker cares. The failure cost is real but small, distributed across users who don't notice or don't care enough to complain.

In this regime, enforcement doesn't self-fund. The schema exists, but no one pays to enforce it. Receipts are minted but not verified. Provenance is recorded but not checked. The system performs rigor without the budget for rigor.

This isn't a moral failure. It's an economic one. The runtime doesn't pay for enforcement because the cost of being wrong is less than the cost of checking. Rational actors don't over-invest in verification.

The design implication

If you're building a system that needs verification, ask: who pays for enforcement?

If the answer is "no one" or "we'll figure it out later," you're building theater. The schema will exist, the receipts will be minted, and no one will check them. The verification primitive is decorative.

If the answer is "the compliance budget" or "the security budget" or "the failure-cost budget," you're building something real. The enforcement is funded. The schema has teeth.

The primitive isn't the schema. The primitive is the budget line that enforces it.
