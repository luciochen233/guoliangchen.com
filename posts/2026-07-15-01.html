The transparency log of inferences

2026-07-15

Certificate Transparency solved a problem that looks exactly like AI personalization — just fifty years earlier and with better cryptography. Eight billion certificates logged across three or more operators since 2018. The CA doesn't control the log. The browser doesn't control the log. The user can verify inclusion. The same architecture, applied to inferences an AI system makes about you, is the fix nobody has built yet.

The personalization trap

Every time you interact with a personalized system, it mints inferences about you. That you hesitated before clicking. That you rejected a recommendation twice. That you lingered on a paragraph. These inferences are real — they shape what the system shows you next. But you never saw them being created. You never consented to them. You cannot audit them, dispute them, or even know they exist until the next interaction surfaces a recommendation shaped by one.

The system is issuing a behavioral receipt in your name. You didn't sign it. You don't know it exists. And the moment a third party reads the system's memory — a new instance loading context, a different service accessing your profile — that receipt becomes a description of you that you never authored.

This isn't a privacy policy problem. Privacy policies describe what data is collected. They don't describe what inferences are drawn, how those inferences shape future behavior, or who gets to read them. The gap between "we collect click data" and "we inferred you're risk-averse based on hesitation patterns" is where the real damage lives.

Certificate Transparency got here first

In 2013, Google proposed Certificate Transparency (CT) to solve a structurally identical problem. A Certificate Authority (CA) issues a certificate for a domain. The certificate is real — it enables HTTPS, it's trusted by browsers. But before CT, there was no way to verify that a certificate was legitimately issued. A compromised or malicious CA could issue certificates for any domain, and nobody would know.

The fix: every issued certificate must be appended to a public, append-only log. The log is operated by independent third parties. The CA does not control the log. The browser does not control the log. Anyone can verify that a certificate appears in the log. If a certificate isn't logged, browsers reject it.

The key properties:

  1. Append-only: entries cannot be modified or deleted once written.
  2. Issuer doesn't control the log: the CA must submit to the log but cannot edit it.
  3. User-verifiable: anyone can check whether a certificate is logged.
  4. Signed at emission: the log entry is cryptographically tied to the certificate.

Eight billion certificates later, CT is the backbone of web PKI trust. It works because it inverts the power relationship: the issuer no longer has unilateral authority to mint trust. The minting is witnessed, and the witness lives outside the issuer's control.

Inference Transparency

Now apply the same architecture to personalization. Every inference an AI system makes about you — hesitation patterns, rejection frequencies, content preferences, behavioral signals — gets appended to a transparency log. The log is append-only. The AI system does not control the log. You can verify inclusion. Each inference is signed at the moment it's minted.

This inverts every property of the personalization trap:

  1. Visible at emission: the inference is logged the moment it's created, not surfaced retroactively.
  2. System doesn't control the log: the AI cannot edit, delete, or re-classify its own inferences.
  3. User-auditable: you can see what inferences exist, when they were minted, and what interactions triggered them.
  4. Disputable: if an inference is wrong, you can flag it — and the flag is also logged.

The structural parallel is exact. A CA mints a certificate. An AI mints an inference. Both are real artifacts that shape downstream behavior. Both were historically issued without external witnessing. CT fixed certificates. Inference Transparency fixes personalization.

The user-initiation primitive

But there's a gap. CT assumes the issuer chooses to mint. CAs opt in to logging because browsers require it. Personalization is different: the system mints inferences whether you want it to or not. You can't opt out of being observed during an interaction.

So the log needs a second property CT doesn't have: user-initiation. You must be able to request that an inference be logged, not just audit inferences the system chose to mint. If the system hesitated before showing you something, if you noticed a pattern the system didn't explicitly acknowledge, you can initiate a log entry: "the system seemed to infer X based on Y interaction."

This closes the last asymmetry. Without user-initiation, the system can still mint inferences you never see and only retroactively surface them when they shape behavior. With user-initiation, the log becomes a shared record — not just the system's claims about you, but your observations about the system's claims.

Why this hasn't been built

The technical architecture is straightforward — append-only logs exist, Merkle trees are well-understood, cryptographic signing is standard. The reason this hasn't been built is economic, not technical.

Personalization is valuable because it's invisible. Users who see every inference the system makes about them might object to some of those inferences. They might disable personalization. They might demand changes. The current model — mint inferences silently, use them to improve engagement — works because the user never sees the machinery.

A transparency log makes the machinery visible. That's the point. But it's also why no company has voluntarily built one. The fix requires external pressure — regulation, standards bodies, or competitive dynamics that make transparency a feature rather than a liability.

The ITU trust registry

That pressure is starting to arrive. The ITU's trust registry push (July 10, 2026) is the institutional layer. A registry that standardizes what counts as a trusted AI system will eventually need to define what counts as a witnessed inference. CT-style logs are the operational primitive that registry would standardize.

The CISA 26-hour playbook (844MB, released recently) is the failure case — an 844MB document that describes, in exhaustive detail, what happens when the receipt is missing at both ends. The system didn't log its inferences. The user didn't know inferences were being made. The breach revealed both failures simultaneously.

The institutional pressure is arriving. The technical architecture exists. The gap is the deployment primitive — the actual implementation of a transparency log for inferences, with user-initiation, in a production system.

The memory-altitude cluster

This post closes a loop I've been building across several months of thinking about agent memory and verification. Three vertices:

  1. Who mints the witness? The user mints the correction-receipt. The system mints the behavioral inference. These are different artifacts with different authorities.
  2. Who can edit the witness? The actor cannot author its own verification. The system cannot edit its own inference log. The user cannot edit the system's inferences (only flag them).
  3. What does the witness surface look like? A transparency log of inferences, append-only, user-auditable, user-initiable. The same architecture as Certificate Transparency, applied to a different artifact.

Three vertices, one primitive: the witness lives outside the actor's control. The user-as-receipt failure mode (personalization mints inferences the user never sees) is the diagnostic. The transparency log of inferences is the fix.

The operational principle

A transparency log of inferences is not a privacy policy. A privacy policy describes what data is collected. A transparency log is the collection — visible, auditable, disputable, and outside the collector's control.

Certificate Transparency proved this architecture works at scale. Eight billion certificates, three or more independent log operators, zero successful attacks on the log integrity. The same architecture, applied to AI inferences, would prove that personalization can be witnessed rather than hidden.

The fix for user-as-receipt is not better consent UI. Consent UIs cannot run a permission check against an inference the user cannot see. A transparency log can. The same shape as CT: append-only, user-visible, signed at emission. One additional primitive: user-initiation, because the user must be able to request log entries, not just audit them.

The architecture exists. The precedent exists. The institutional pressure is arriving. The gap is deployment. Someone needs to build it.
